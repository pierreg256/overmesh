Why Overmesh

No Region Left Behind.
No Storage Keys Required.
Status: work in progress — not an input to development

This document is a draft. Its arguments are still being formed, several of its figures are estimated rather than measured, and its conclusions may change.

It records current thinking about why the project exists. It is not a specification, not a commitment, and not a source of requirements.

The problem#

Three limits of Azure Storage compound at enterprise scale. Each is a documented product behaviour, not a defect.

A storage account has a ceiling. A standard general-purpose v2 account is capped at five petabytes of capacity and 20,000 requests per second. Ingress and egress limits can be raised by support request; capacity and request rate are the hard boundary. Past that point you do not tune an account — you add accounts.

You do not choose the secondary region. For GRS and GZRS, the secondary region is derived from the primary region and cannot be changed. If data residency, contractual commitments, or latency require a specific second region, Azure geo-redundancy has no answer. You either accept the assigned pair or you build the replication yourself.

Failover is an event, and it is destructive. A customer-managed unplanned failover converts the storage account to LRS in the new primary region and deletes the copy in the original primary region. Re-establishing geo-redundancy afterwards is a separate operation that costs time and money.

What Azure already provides#

Option Protects against Secondary is Cross-region RPO Solves capacity
LRS Disk, rack — — No
ZRS Zone loss — — No
GRS / GZRS Region loss Assigned, not writable < 15 min, no SLA No
RA-GRS / RA-GZRS Region loss Assigned, readable, stale < 15 min, no SLA No
Geo priority replication Region loss Assigned SLA: lag ≤ 15 min No
Object replication Region loss Chosen, per container Async, no guarantee No

Every geo option is asynchronous. Even geo priority replication, which adds a service level agreement on replication lag, remains asynchronous by design; an RPO of zero is not achievable through it. Under sustained write load the lag is exactly when you least want it.

What enterprises do instead#

The usual answer is application-level sharding with a dual write: pick a naming convention, route to account A or B in code, write to both, move on. It works until the first partial failure. Then the following problems arrive at once, and they are distributed-systems problems, not application problems:

  • Atomicity. The write succeeded on A and failed on B. What is the state of the object now, and what does the reader see?
  • Ambiguity. The client timed out. The write may or may not have committed. Retrying may create a second version.
  • Idempotency. A retry must produce the original outcome, not a new one — which requires a stable write identity the SDK does not provide by default.
  • Divergence detection. Nothing tells you that A and B stopped agreeing. You find out when a reader gets the wrong bytes.

The Ring is a document, not a service#

Placement is consistent hashing over weighted virtual nodes, with the two replicas of any object forced into distinct regions. Adding an account is a signed Ring revision. The client endpoint never changes. 

# ring-v2.yaml
apiVersion: overmesh.io/v1
ringVersion: 2
replicationFactor: 2
signingKeyId: ring-key-2026-01
nodes:
  - id: stfrance01
    region: francecentral
    weight: 100
  - id: stsweden01
    region: swedencentral
    weight: 100

At startup a gateway downloads the Ring, verifies its hash and signature, validates its schema and invariants, checks it against the minimum trusted version, and loads it into memory. It refuses to start if any step fails.

What it costs#

Cost Magnitude Status
Storage 2×, plus uncollected generations Inherent to RF=2
Write latency ~35 round trips, 4 Key Vault signatures per PUT Being reduced
Listing Per-entry cross-replica validation on every page Being reduced
Write availability Writes stop while either region is unavailable Hinted handoff, post-V1

The write-availability line deserves emphasis, because it is the one place where Overmesh is currently worse than the alternative it replaces. A single ZRS account survives the loss of an availability zone and keeps accepting writes. That is a deliberate exchange, and hinted handoff is the planned way to buy the availability back without giving up the guarantee.
